The construction of a lunar colony requiring $10^8$ metric tons of cargo represents humanity's most ambitious logistical undertaking, presenting a "Logistics Trilemma" between minimizing construction time, controlling astronomical costs, and mitigating environmental damage. To navigate this complex landscape, we develop a comprehensive \textbf{Integrated Space Logistics Framework (ISLF)} that evolves from an idealized blueprint to a robust, life-sustaining, and ecologically responsible reality.

\textbf{For Task 1,} The Blueprint (Logistics Architecture Optimization). We begin by establishing a theoretical baseline under ideal conditions. Rejecting traditional static cost assumptions, we employ a \textbf{Dynamic Cost Engine} and construct a \textbf{Hybrid Logistics Optimization Model (HLOM)}. Our analysis reveals a convex "V-curve" in project duration, identifying a \textbf{Golden Load Ratio ($\alpha^* \approx 0.32$)} specifically optimized for the \textbf{minimum possible completion time}. By assigning 32\% of the cargo to the Space Elevator and 68\% to Rockets, we leverage the "Marginal Time-Value Arbitrage," achieving this theoretical minimum of \textbf{61.3 years}—saving 30 years compared to rocket-only strategies while reducing costs by an order of magnitude.

\textbf{For Task 2,} The Stress Test (Stochastic Robustness). Moving from theory to reality, we expose our blueprint to the friction of Stochastic Failures. We construct a \textbf{Four-Layer Stochastic Framework}, modeling elevator burst downtime and rocket pad resets. Monte Carlo simulations reveal that static plans suffer from "Heavy Tail" risks. To counteract this, we design an \textbf{Adaptive Surge Policy}. Optimization proves that a surge capacity of \textbf{$\gamma^* \approx 1.8$} (80\% backup intensity) is the critical "shock absorber" required to guarantee the deadline with \textbf{95\% confidence}, effectively suppressing failure variance.

\textbf{For Task 3,} With the colony's gross metabolic demand reaching 6.39 million tons annually, we integrate ECLSS recycling ($\eta = 0.98$) to mitigate the net logistical burden to a manageable $\sim$0.13 million tons/year. However, the risk shifts from volume to volatility: given the zero tolerance for dehydration, our Dual-Threshold Inventory Policy reveals that a 30-day safety stock is the minimum buffer required to survive the "heavy tail" supply disruptions identified in Model II.

\textbf{For Task 4,} The Conscience (Environmental Impact Assessment). Finally, we audit the ecological cost of our robust system. Coupling the logistics engine with a \textbf{Stratospheric Leaky Bucket Model}, we uncover a fundamental \textbf{"Pollution Shift"}: elevators shift the burden from operational soot to construction carbon debt. Crucially, we identify a \textbf{Synergistic Frontier} where high elevator utilization minimizes both financial cost and the \textbf{Environmental Damage Index (EDI)}. However, a "Haste Penalty" exists: compressing the timeline below 40 years triggers exponential damage, advocating for a "Patient Construction" strategy.

\textbf{Bottom line:} Our sequential analysis defines a structured strategic evolution. Using a hybrid logistics blueprint with the time-optimal ratio 0.32, we achieve a 61.3-year minimum construction timeline. After core infrastructure completion, the system transitions to an elevator-dominant phase for long-term sustainment, minimizing the Environmental Damage Index. Our framework confirms that while speed enables colony construction, reliability—not speed—is the fundamental currency of the lunar economy.

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Problem Background}

\begin{quote}
\textit{``Earth is the cradle of humanity, but one cannot live in a cradle forever.''}
\hfill --- Konstantin Tsiolkovsky
\end{quote}

The transition from a single-planet species to a multi-planetary civilization represents the next great leap in human evolution. Establishing a permanent lunar presence is not merely an act of exploration but a necessity for relieving the ecological pressure on Earth’s fragile ecosystem. By 2050, the ambition to establish a lunar colony for 100,000 residents marks a turning point in history, transforming space from a frontier of discovery into a domain of habitation and commerce. However, the physical reality of this ambition confronts a staggering logistical barrier: the requirement to transport \textbf{100 million metric tons ($10^8$ tons)} of infrastructure and supplies across the gravity well.

The Lunar Colony Administration faces a critical strategic crossroads defined by two competing technological paradigms. On one hand, traditional \textbf{Chemical Rocketry}, exemplified by heavy-lift launch vehicles, offers a mature and flexible solution but is plagued by high marginal costs and significant atmospheric emissions. On the other hand, the emerging \textbf{Space Elevator System}, consisting of three equatorial Galactic Harbours, promises a revolution in efficiency with low-cost, electric-driven tether climbers, yet it is constrained by fixed throughput limits and immense upfront complexity.

The challenge is not simply moving mass; it is resolving a ``Logistics Trilemma''—the inherent conflict between minimizing construction duration, controlling astronomical financial costs, and mitigating irreversible environmental damage to Earth. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:concept}, the choice between these transport modes will dictate the economic viability and ecological sustainability of humanity's expansion into the solar system.

\subsection{Restatement of the Problem}

Our team is tasked with developing a comprehensive **Integrated Space Logistics Framework (ISLF)** to determine the optimal roadmap for establishing and sustaining the lunar colony. We must navigate the trade-offs between the Space Elevator’s high efficiency and Rocketry’s high scalability while accounting for real-world stochastic frictions and environmental ethics. Specifically, our modeling effort focuses on the following four core tasks:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Logistics Architecture Optimization:} Analyze three transport scenarios (Elevator-only, Rocket-only, and Hybrid) to determine the optimal resource allocation that minimizes cost and construction time for the 100 million ton cargo requirement.
    \item \textbf{Resilience under Uncertainty:} Move beyond ideal conditions to assess how the system performs under stochastic disruptions, such as tether instability, launch failures, and weather delays, and determine the impact on the project timeline.
    \item \textbf{Supply Chain Sustainability (The Water Lifeline):} Investigate the rigid demand of sustaining the colony once operational. Specifically, model the supply chain for one year of water consumption, accounting for recycling technologies (ECLSS) and inventory risks to prevent catastrophic shortages.
    \item \textbf{Environmental Impact Assessment:} Quantify the ecological footprint of the proposed solutions on Earth’s atmosphere. We must propose adjustments to the model to minimize environmental damage (e.g., stratospheric soot and carbon emissions) while maintaining logistical feasibility.
\end{itemize}

Ultimately, we are required to synthesize these findings into a strategic memorandum for the Lunar Colony Administration, recommending a robust course of action for the colony's establishment.

\section{Assumptions and Justifications}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.5em]
  \item \textbf{\large Assumption I:} The project timeline commences in 2050, and the total mass demand of 100 million tons ($M_{total}$) accounts for all necessary structural components, neglecting initial in-situ resource utilization (ISRU) savings. \\
  \textbf{\large Justification:} Assuming a 2050 start provides a realistic timeframe for the maturation of critical precursor technologies like commercial fusion and graphene manufacturing. Treating the demand as fixed and conservative ensures that our feasibility analysis accounts for the worst-case logistical load, providing a robust upper bound for infrastructure requirements.

  \item \textbf{\large Assumption II:} The Two-Stage Transport Topology. We assume the Space Elevator (Galactic Harbour) operates as a synchronized two-stage system: cargo is first lifted to the Apex Anchor via tether, then transferred to the Moon via tugs. The tether's lift rate $C_E$ is the binding constraint of the entire link.\\
  \textbf{\large Justification:} Feasibility Simplification. While the physical mechanics of climbing are continuous, the system's throughput is dictated by the slowest segment. Assuming the tether is the bottleneck allows us to model the elevator as a flow channel with capacity $C_E$, decoupling it from the complex orbital mechanics of the Apex transfer tugs.

  \item \textbf{\large Assumption III:} Rocket launch costs follow a technological learning curve (Wright’s Law) decaying at 5\% every 5 years, while Space Elevator operational capacity remains linear once built. \\
  \textbf{\large Justification:} Historical aerospace data supports the use of learning curves to model cost reductions over time. Assuming linear capacity for the elevator simplifies the steady-state analysis, allowing us to focus on the long-term trade-offs between the high-frequency but costly rocket launches and the high-volume, low-marginal-cost elevator system.

  \item \textbf{\large Assumption IV:} Mechanical failures in the transportation system follow a Poisson process with exponential inter-arrival times, and repair times are exponentially distributed. \\
  \textbf{\large Justification:} The "Memoryless Property" of the Poisson process is a standard reliability engineering assumption for complex systems where failure risk does not strictly depend on age. Exponential repair times capture the "long tail" risk of rare, catastrophic failures that disrupt the supply chain significantly more than routine maintenance, which is critical for our resilience analysis.

  \item \textbf{\large Assumption V:} Decarbonization of green energy. We assume that the carbon intensity of the Space Elevator's operation is a function of the Earth's grid mix $\chi$ (Green Penetration Rate), which can improve over time. \\
  \textbf{\large Justification:} LCA Boundary Definition. The Elevator itself emits nothing locally. Its environmental footprint is entirely indirect (Scope 2 emissions). Linking it to the grid mix $\chi$ allows us to model the sensitivity of environmental impact to energy policy.

  \item \textbf{\large Assumption VI:} Stratospheric Black Carbon accumulation follows a "Leaky Bucket" dynamic model with a residence time of approximately 4 years, and global mixing is instantaneous. \\
  \textbf{\large Justification:} Physical accumulation is the primary driver of long-term environmental damage. The "Leaky Bucket" model captures the cumulative debt of pollutants that injection rates exceeding natural deposition create. Instantaneous mixing simplifies the atmospheric physics to a tractable global variable ($S_{BC}$), sufficient for estimating the order of magnitude of radiative forcing risks without running a full climate simulation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Model I: The Hybrid Logistics Optimization Model (HLOM)}
\subsection{Model Overview \& Logical Framework}
To resolve the "Logistics Trilemma"---the conflict between minimizing construction time, controlling astronomical costs, and overcoming physical capacity limits---we propose the Hybrid Logistics Optimization Model (HLOM). Unlike traditional static models, HLOM incorporates a Dynamic Cost Engine driven by technological learning curves and utilizes a Concurrent Schedule Optimization to identify the optimal resource allocation.

The computational framework follows four distinct phases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Initialization}: Define physical constraints ($M=10^8$ tons) and initialize the dynamic cost functions ($C_L(t)$).
    \item \textbf{Baseline Evaluation}: Assess the feasibility boundaries of mono-modal strategies (Scenario A \& B).
    \item \textbf{Hybrid Optimization}: Employ an iterative $\alpha$-scanning algorithm to minimize the objective function.
    \item \textbf{Robustness Check}: Perform sensitivity analysis using the Architecture Factor ($k$) and generate the Pareto Frontier.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Mechanical Modeling of Subsystems}
Before optimizing the hybrid strategy, we must mathematically define the cost and capacity behaviors of the two distinct transport modes.

The Space Elevator (SE) provides low-marginal-cost transport but is strictly limited by the throughput of the three Galactic Harbours.

\noindent \textbf{Capacity Constraint}:\\
Let $C_{SE}$ be the fixed annual system capacity ($3 \times 179,000$ tons). The time to transport mass $M$ is linear:
\[
T_A = \frac{M}{C_{SE}} \approx 186.22 \ \text{Years}
\]

\noindent \textbf{Cost Structure}:\\
The cost consists of the tether lift (OPEX) plus the transfer from the Apex Anchor to the Moon. Unlike Earth-surface launches, the Apex release provides significant initial orbital energy. To model this rigorously, we introduce the \textbf{Apex Transfer Payload} ($q_{Apex}$), which may differ from Earth-launch payloads due to vacuum engine optimization.

The total number of required transfer trips $N_{\text{trips}}$ is calculated as:
\[
N_{\text{trips}} = \left\lceil \frac{M}{q_{Apex}} \right\rceil
\]

Consequently, the Total Cost $Z_A$ is formulated as:
\[
Z_A = \underbrace{M \cdot c_E}_{\text{Elevator OPEX}} + \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{trips}}} \left( \beta \cdot C_L(y_i) \right)}_{\text{Apex-Moon Transfer}}
\]
Where $\beta \in [0.02, 0.1]$ represents the \textbf{Effective Cost Ratio}. This factor aggregates the dual benefits of minimal $\Delta v$ requirements for Trans-Lunar Injection (TLI) and increased payload mass fraction compared to surface launches.


Rockets offer scalable throughput but face high unit costs. We reject the assumption of static costs and introduce a Learning Curve Model.

\noindent \textbf{Dynamic Cost Engine}:\\
The unit launch cost $C_L(y)$ decays exponentially due to technological maturation (e.g., reusability), bounded by a Asymptotic Cost Floor $C_{\text{floor}}$:
\[
C_L(y) = \maxfunc{C_{\text{floor}}}{C_{2050} \cdot (1 - \rho)^{\floor{\frac{y - 2050}{5}}}}
\]
Where $\rho=0.05$ represents a 5\% cost reduction every 5 years.

\noindent \textbf{Throughput \& Time}:\\
With $K$ sites and frequency $r$, the annual capacity is $\mathcal{R} = 365 \cdot K \cdot r \cdot q$.
\[
T_B = \frac{M}{\mathcal{R}}, \quad Z_B = \sum_{t=0}^{T_B} \mathcal{R} \cdot C_L(2050 + t)
\]


\subsection{The Hybrid Optimization Framework}
Scenario C integrates both systems. We define a decision variable $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ as the Elevator Load Ratio, representing the fraction of total mass $M$ assigned to the Space Elevator.


Since both systems operate simultaneously, the project completion time $T_C$ is determined by the "slowest link" (the bottleneck):
\[
T_C(\alpha) = \maxfunc{\underbrace{\frac{\alpha M}{C_{SE}}}_{T_{\text{Elevator}}}}{\underbrace{\frac{(1-\alpha)M}{\mathcal{R}}}_{T_{\text{Rocket}}}}
\]

Our primary objective in Model I is to identify the \textbf{Time-Optimal Allocation} $\alpha^*$ that minimizes the total project make-span $T_C$. By minimizing the "idling waste" where one system finishes long before the other, we maximize the utilization efficiency of the combined infrastructure:
\[
\min_{\alpha} \quad T_C(\alpha)
\]
Simultaneously, we calculate the associated Total Cost $Z(\alpha)$ to evaluate the economic implications of this maximum-speed strategy:
\[
Z(\alpha) = Z_A(\alpha M) + Z_B((1-\alpha)M)
\]


The minimum possible time ($T_{\text{min}}$) occurs at the \textbf{Load Matching Point}, where the Space Elevator and Rocket systems complete their transport tasks simultaneously ($T_{\text{Elevator}} = T_{\text{Rocket}}$). Setting the two terms in the max function equal, we derive the analytical solution:
\[
\frac{\alpha^* M}{C_{SE}} = \frac{(1-\alpha^*)M}{\mathcal{R}} \implies \alpha^* = \frac{C_{SE}}{C_{SE} + \mathcal{R}}
\]
Substituting the baseline parameters ($r=2, q=150$), we find the time-optimal load ratio to be $\alpha^* \approx 0.32$. This result confirms that to achieve the fastest construction speed, the system must leverage the high throughput of rockets despite their higher cost.

\section{Model II: Stochastic Robustness \& Adaptive Control System}
\subsection{Uncertainty Modeling \& Logic Framework}
The optimization in Model I assumes "perfect working order." However, real-world aerospace logistics are strictly governed by Stochastic Failures: Space Elevator (SE) tethers suffer from burst downtime due to oscillations, and Rocket launch pads require reset time ($\tau_{\text{reset}}$) after failures. In Model II, we construct a Four-Layer Stochastic Framework to quantify these risks and design an Adaptive Control Policy.

We model the SE state as a Two-State Continuous Markov Chain:
\begin{itemize}
  \item State UP: Nominal throughput $C_E$.
  \item State DOWN: Zero throughput.
\end{itemize}
Defined by failure rate $\lambda_E$ and repair rate $\mu_E$, the Steady-State Availability ($a_E$) is derived as:
\[
a_E = \frac{\mu_E}{\lambda_E + \mu_E} = \frac{1}{1 + \lambda_E \cdot \text{MTTR}_E}
\]
where $\text{MTTR}_E = 1/\mu_E$ is the Mean Time To Repair. Consequently, the Effective SE Throughput ($\tilde{C}_E$) is the nominal capacity discounted by availability:
\[
\tilde{C}_E = a_E \cdot C_E
\]

Rocket launches are modeled as Bernoulli trials with success probability $s=1-p_R$. Unlike simple probability discounting, we introduce a \textbf{Penalty Reset Mechanism}: a failure locks the launch pad for $\tau_{\text{reset}}$ days.

To derive the Effective Launch Frequency ($f_{\text{eff}}$), we first define the expected number of successes ($E[S_r]$) and the expected cycle length ($E[L]$) for a single site per day:
\[
E[S_r] = \sum_{i=1}^{r} s^i, \quad E[L] = 1 + \tau_{\text{reset}}(1 - s^r)
\]
Where $1 - s^r$ represents the probability that at least one failure occurs in $r$ attempts, triggering the reset time.

For computational tractability, we approximate the expected successes using the closed-form sum of the geometric series:
\[
E[S_r] \approx \frac{s(1 - s^r)}{1 - s}
\]
This analytical smoothing allows us to treat the daily launch rate $r$ as a \textbf{continuous decision variable} in the optimization layer. Consequently, the effective frequency (launches/day/site) is formulated as:
\[
f_{\text{eff}}(r, p_R, \tau_{\text{reset}}) = \frac{\frac{s(1 - s^r)}{1 - s}}{1 + \tau_{\text{reset}}(1 - s^r)}
\]

Incorporating weather availability ($a_B$) and payload ($q$), the Effective Rocket Throughput ($\tilde{C}_R$) is:
\[
\tilde{C}_R = 365 \cdot K \cdot a_B \cdot q \cdot f_{\text{eff}}
\]

Under stochastic conditions, the optimal mass allocation $\alpha^*$ (Load Matching Point) drifts to balance the effective capacities:
\[
\alpha^* = \frac{\tilde{C}_E}{\tilde{C}_E + \tilde{C}_R}
\]

\subsection{Layer B: Feasibility Region \& Capacity Gap}
We define the Feasibility Region as the set of throughput combinations that satisfy the target completion time $T_{\text{target}}$. The mathematical necessary condition is:
\[
\tilde{C}_E + \tilde{C}_R \ge \frac{M}{T_{\text{target}}}
\]
To quantify the severity of risk, we calculate the Capacity Gap ($\Delta C$) for any given risk profile $S$:
\[
\Delta C(S) = \max\left(0, \ \frac{M}{T_{\text{target}}} - (\tilde{C}_E(S) + \tilde{C}_R(S))\right)
\]
A positive $\Delta C$ indicates that without strategic intervention, the system expects to miss the deadline.

    \item \texttt{elevator\_down\_days}: If $>0$, SE throughput is zero.
    \item \texttt{pad\_down\_days[$i$]}: If $>0$, the $i$-th launch site is locked.
\end{itemize}

We first evaluate \textbf{Policy P0 (Fixed Rocket Schedule)}. Under this policy, the logistics system dynamically routes cargo to any currently available transport mode, but the rocket fleet strictly adheres to a pre-set launch cadence ($r_{\text{base}}$) regardless of the backlog size.

\noindent \textbf{Mechanism of Delay}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inelastic Supply}: When the Space Elevator fails (State DOWN), the rocket fleet continues to operate at the nominal rate $r_{\text{base}}$. They do \textbf{not} surge to compensate for the lost elevator capacity.

\section{Model III: Water Supply Chain Resilience \& Cost Analysis}

\subsection{The Stochastic Inventory Dynamics }
We model the lunar water reservoir as a Discrete-Time Stochastic Inventory System to handle supply interruptions.

Let $W_t$ denote the water inventory level (tons) at the end of day $t$. The system state evolves according to the mass balance equation:
\[
W_{t+1} = W_t + x_{E,t} + x_{R,t} - d_{\text{day}}, \quad \forall t \in \{0, \dots, 364\}
\]
Subject to the constraints $W_t \ge 0$ (non-negative inventory) and delivery limits:
\[
0 \le x_{E,t} \le c_{E,\text{day}}^{(w)}, \quad 0 \le x_{R,t} \le c_{R,\text{day}}^{(w)}
\]
Where $x_{E,t}$ and $x_{R,t}$ are the actual delivered quantities from the Space Elevator and Rockets, conditioned by the stochastic availability states derived in Model II.

We implement an Order-up-to Policy defined by two strategic parameters: Safety Stock Days ($L_{\text{safe}}$) and Buffer Days ($B_{\text{buffer}}$).

\noindent\textbf{Safety Threshold ($s$)}: A strategic reserve to survive the longest expected elevator downtime ($MTTR_{max}$):
\[
s = L_{\text{safe}} \cdot d_{\text{day}}
\]
\textbf{Target Threshold ($S$)}: The operational target to smooth out arrival jitters:
\[
S = (L_{\text{safe}} + B_{\text{buffer}}) \cdot d_{\text{day}}
\]
\textbf{Replenishment Decision}: The logistics system generates a daily plan $x_t^{\text{plan}}$ to restore inventory to $S$, prioritizing the Space Elevator due to lower marginal cost:
\[
x_t^{\text{plan}} = \min \left( c_{E,\text{day}}^{(w)} + c_{R,\text{day}}^{(w)}, \ \max(0, S - W_t) \right)
\]
\textbf{Allocation logic}:
\[
x_{E,t} = \min(x_t^{\text{plan}}, c_{E,\text{day}}^{(w)}), \quad x_{R,t} = x_t^{\text{plan}} - x_{E,t}
\]

\noindent\textbf{Parameter Calibration}: To ensure robust performance, we calibrated the policy parameters based on the reliability data from Model II:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Operational Buffer ($B_{\text{buffer}} = 15$ days)}: Designed to absorb high-frequency variances (e.g., launch delays), effectively decoupling the daily order cycle from transient noise.
    \item \textbf{Strategic Safety Stock ($L_{\text{safe}} = 30$ days)}: Acts as the "Last Line of Defense" to survive long-duration elevator outages ($MTTR \approx 14$ days).
\end{itemize}

\section{Model IV: Integrated Environmental \& Operational Simulation (IEOS)}
\subsection{Problem Abstraction \& System State}
To comprehensively evaluate the trade-offs between construction speed, operational reliability, and environmental sustainability, we developed the Integrated Environmental \& Operational Simulation (IEOS). Unlike static assessments, IEOS is a discrete-time, multi-phase stochastic engine that couples logistics capacity with an "Environmental Ledger."

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Time}: Discrete days indexed by $t = 0, 1, 2, \dots$, corresponding to the global clock $t_{\text{global}}$.
    \item \textbf{Launch Sites}: Indexed by $k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$, representing individual rocket pads.
\end{itemize}

We define the system configuration using a decision vector $\pi$, which is subjected to Monte Carlo sweeping:
\[
\pi = (K, r, \chi, s_E)
\]
Where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $K \in \mathbb{Z}^+$: Number of active rocket launch sites ($K_{\text{pads}}$).
    \item $r \in \mathbb{R}^+$: Daily launch intensity per site (scheduled attempts, $r_{\text{rate}}$).
    \item $\chi \in [0, 1]$: Grid Decarbonization Ratio, representing the cleanliness of the energy mix ($\chi_{\text{green}}$).
    \item $s_E \in [0, 1]$: Elevator Capacity Scaling Factor ($s_{\text{elev\_scale}}$).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Stochastic Capacity Engines}
At each time step $t$, the system generates available capacity vectors based on infrastructure reliability.

The elevator's state is governed by a Burst-Failure Process. Let $S^E_t$ be the remaining repair days. The daily effective elevator capacity $m^E_t$ is determined by:
\[
m^E_t = 
\begin{cases} 
0 & \text{if } S^E_t > 0 \text{ (Under Repair)} \\
0 & \text{if } U \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1) < (1 - e^{-\lambda}) \text{ (New Failure)} \\
C_E \cdot s_E & \text{otherwise (Operational)}
\end{cases}
\]
If a failure occurs, repair time is sampled from an exponential distribution: $S^E_{t+1} \leftarrow \lceil \text{Exp}(\mu) \rceil$.

Rockets are modeled as independent parallel servers with a Penalty Reset mechanism. For each pad $k$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{State Check}: If pad $k$ is in cooldown (pad\_down\_days $> 0$), it remains inactive.
    \item \textbf{Weather Check}: Launch is permitted only if weather condition $A_B = 1$.
    \item \textbf{Attempt Generation}: The scheduled attempts $n^{\text{att}}_k$ follow a deterministic-plus-stochastic rule:
    \[
    n^{\text{att}}_k = \lfloor r \rfloor + \mathbb{I}(U < (r - \lfloor r \rfloor))
    \]
    \item \textbf{Outcome}: Each attempt succeeds with probability $P_{\text{succ}}$. A failure triggers a "Lockout," setting the pad's state to $\tau_{\text{reset}}$.
\end{itemize}
The total daily rocket capacity $m^R_t$ is the sum of successful payloads:
\[
m^R_t = \sum_{k=1}^{K} s_{t,k} \cdot q
\]
Where $s_{t,k}$ is the success count for pad $k$, and $q$ is the standard payload ($q_{\text{rocket\_payload\_ton}}$).

\subsection{Multi-Phase Sequential Dynamics}
The simulation proceeds through two distinct phases, linking construction to operation.

The objective is to transport the infrastructure mass $M^{\text{build}}_{\text{target}}$ ($10^8$ tons). The system operates in "Maximum Effort" mode:
\[
B_{t+1} = B_t + m^E_t + m^R_t
\]
The phase concludes at $T_{\text{build}}$ when $B_t \ge M^{\text{build}}_{\text{target}}$. The total emissions from this phase constitute the Construction Carbon Debt ($E_{\text{build}}$).

Upon construction completion, the system switches to water logistics for the colony population $P=10^5$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Demand}: $D = P \cdot w$ (17,500 tons/day).
    \item \textbf{Safety Target}: $W^\star = D \cdot L_{\text{safe}}$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{Phase 2a: Startup Fill (Eq. StartUp-Fill)} \\
The system accumulates initial stock without consumption until $W_t \ge W^\star$:
\[
W_{t+1} = W_t + m^E_t + m^R_t
\]

\noindent \textbf{Phase 2b: Steady-State Operation} \\
We implement an Order-up-to Policy to maintain water security.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gap Calculation (Eq. Op-Gap)}:
    \[
    g_t = \max(0, W^\star - W_t + D)
    \]
    \item \textbf{Capacity Allocation (Eq. Op-Alloc)}: Priority is given to the elevator to minimize costs.
    \[
    x^E_t = \min(g_t, m^E_t), \quad x^R_t = \min(g_t - x^E_t, m^R_t)
    \]
    \item \textbf{Inventory Dynamics (Eq. Inv-Dyn)}:
    \[
    W_{t+1} = \max(0, W_t + (x^E_t + x^R_t) - D)
    \]
    If $W_{t+1} = 0$, a Stockout Event is recorded.
    \item \textbf{Service Level (Eq. Service-Level)}: \\
    The annual service level is calculated as:
    \[
    \text{SL} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{365} \text{demand\_met}_t}{\sum_{t=1}^{365} D}
    \]
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Environmental Ledger \& Evaluation}
We integrate an accounting system (EnvLedger) to track cumulative impacts.

Total $\text{CO}_2$-equivalent emissions are aggregated daily. We distinguish between construction and operational phases to capture the "Pollution Shift":
\[
E_{\text{CO2}}(t) = E_{\text{build}} + \sum_{\tau=T_{\text{build}}}^{t} \left( a_\tau \cdot e_{\text{launch}} + m^E_\tau \cdot e_{\text{grid}}(\chi) \right)
\]
The operational increment is $E_{\text{op}} = E_{\text{cum,end}} - E_{\text{cum,start}}$.

Stratospheric Black Carbon (BC) accumulates and decays over time. The stock $S_{\text{bc}}(t)$ evolves as:
\[
S_{\text{bc}}(t+1) = S_{\text{bc}}(t) \cdot (1 - \delta) + u(t)
\]
where $\delta$ is the decay rate and $u(t)$ is the daily injection from rockets. The peak risk is $S_{\max} = \max_t S_{\text{bc}}(t)$.

\section{Conclusion}
We have developed the \textbf{Integrated Space Logistics Framework (ISLF)}, a multi-layered system coupling dynamic cost optimization, stochastic resilience analysis, and environmental impact assessment. Our research confirms that a \textbf{Hybrid Strategy} (assigning 32\% load to Space Elevators) unlocks the theoretical minimum construction time of \textbf{61.3 years}, yet requires an \textbf{Adaptive Surge Policy} ($\gamma=1.8$) to neutralize the "heavy tail" risks of mechanical failures. Crucially, we identified a fundamental "Pollution Shift": while rockets are necessary for speed, they incur an exponential environmental penalty, whereas the Space Elevator serves as the "Green Lifeline" capable of minimizing the Environmental Damage Index (EDI) by an order of magnitude. Consequently, we recommend the Administration execute a phased approach—utilizing the Hybrid Surge for rapid establishment followed by an immediate pivot to an \textbf{Elevator-Dominant} supply chain for operations. This strategy successfully balances the "Logistics Trilemma," proving that while speed establishes the colony, sustainability ensures its survival.
